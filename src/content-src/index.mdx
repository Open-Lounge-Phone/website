## Your people, your rules

There's so much more it can do, and all of it runs on **connections**. Sign in on a public hub
you trust, or federate your own servers and services, and choose the level of safety and
security you want at every step: who can reach you, when you're taking calls, and whether your
connections can see that you're around. Nobody reaches you until you've accepted them, and
there's no phone number to spam.

Kids' phones on the market are closed boxes with a fixed feature list. This one is open: as a
child grows they can learn how it works and tinker with it, while every rule stays with their
guardians until they say otherwise.

## What works today

- **Calls** between phones and the grown-ups' companion app, peer to peer (audio never passes
  through the server).
- **Default-deny contacts:** a guardian decides exactly who can call the phone and who it can call.
- **Quiet hours** per household, with a clear "quiet until 7:00" on the phone.
- **Voicemail everywhere:** a call that isn't answered — no answer, declined, busy, quiet hours —
  goes to a greeting (the standard one, your recorded name, or your own), and the message lands
  in the right inbox, with a transcript.
- **Accounts, passkeys and invites:** your own address (`name@server`), households, teams and
  organizations; add family by link; sign in with a passkey, no passwords or email.
- **Connections across servers ("knock, then talk"):** knock on someone's address on any Open
  Lounge Phone server; once they accept you can call, and a guardian can let them call a kid's
  phone. Blocking, limits and signed server-to-server requests keep it spam-free. No phone
  numbers and no bridge to the phone network, ever.
- **Rooms and 3-way calls:** family party lines and phone rooms with their own address
  (`standup@your.server`) that anyone allowed can drop into — from the app or a phone's key; hold a
  call, add a caller and merge; transfer a call.
- **Lounge phones:** a shared phone anyone takes over as themselves — scan its code, press the key
  it flashes — even people from other servers, and it forgets them afterwards.
- **Any device as a phone:** open `/device/` on an old phone or tablet and add it to the home
  screen.
- **Two ways to run it:** your own Cloudflare account (Workers, Durable Objects, D1, R2) or a
  self-hosted Node server with SQLite.

## A free public hub

Social media is dead, but being social isn't. The hub gives you an address your group and
friends can reach you on, with you deciding who gets through and when. You don't need the
hardware; it just makes a great always-on desk phone: intentional phone time, rather than a
phone that goes everywhere with you.

For people who don't want to run a server, the public hub is **free, funded by donations** —
most calls go straight between the two people, so it costs little to run. It's live: [sign up at hub.openloungephone.app](https://hub.openloungephone.app).

## The hardware (v0.1 early dev board prototype)

One small, deliberately minimal open circuit board built around an ESP32-S3:
- 12 swappable mechanical keys and a hook switch, and a small e-paper status display
- a 3.5 mm jack for an off-the-shelf classic G-style handset
- a piezo ringer, one status light, and USB-C for power and updates (no battery)

**No microphone on the board.** The only microphone is in the handset: unplug the handset and
there is no microphone connected at all, and while it is hung up the software keeps it muted.
The phone is mains/USB-powered like a landline — no battery by design, not a mobile device.

Four phones on the first dev boards, [hardware
v0.1](https://github.com/Open-Lounge-Phone/open-lounge-phone/releases/tag/hw-v0.1) (rev A), are
built and working, here running the experimental [labs build](/extend/labs/) (0.0.0) and signed
in to the public hub. It's lightly tested so far, and full testing waits on the Wi-Fi antennas;
the newest phone firmware is tested in a simulator, and labs features come to it later. The release
has the exact Gerbers, parts lists, schematic and a build guide.

### Board v0.901: wired network and PoE (in design)

The next board revision adds an **RJ45 jack**, so the phone can run on a network cable instead of
Wi-Fi, and **PoE**: one cable for network and power, like a desk phone. USB-C still works, and
either one powers the phone. It adds a WIZnet W5500 Ethernet chip, a jack with built-in
magnetics, and an isolated 802.3af PoE module kept 3 mm from the rest of the board. It's still
one board with no I/O expander: the Ethernet chip shares the display's wires.

It's designed and checked (schematic checks, power budget, full layout with zero design-rule
errors, case fit), but **not built or tested yet**. The phone firmware (since 0.9.5; build it from
source) drives the Ethernet chip: with a cable it uses the cable and turns Wi-Fi off, and without one it
uses Wi-Fi; tested in the simulator so far, not on a board. Parts come to about $18 a board.

**Power:** one Ethernet cable from a PoE switch port or a PoE injector (IEEE 802.3af or 802.3at)
runs the whole phone, network and power together, up to 100 m. Or use a USB-C charger. A
computer's USB port is for flashing and the console only, with the PoE cable plugged in too.
"Passive" 24 V PoE injectors won't power it.
